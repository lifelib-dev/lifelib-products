## Scope and applicability

### The applicability sentence

¶3 (PDF p. 1206) extends the appendix to "all life insurance policies, with or without nonforfeiture values", provided their
issue date is no earlier than the appendix's own effective date, subject to the exceptions and conditions that follow. The
same paragraph then adds an anti-expansion clause: none of it is to be read as widening the reach of the Valuation of Life
Insurance Policies Model Regulation to policies the paragraph exempts.

Two things follow that matter to the library. First, the printed scope is **all life insurance policies**, not "term and ULSG" —
term and ULSG are simply the two designs the exceptions and conditions do not carve out and the calculation paragraphs are built
around. Second, ¶2 (PDF p. 1206) makes the appendix's own construction the statutory method for anything it reaches: the
basic-reserve method the appendix defines "will constitute the Commissioners' Reserve Valuation Method" for every policy within
its scope.

### Effective dates — read this before recording any date

**The appendix nowhere prints a calendar effective date for itself.** Every applicability sentence uses the phrase "the
effective date of this appendix" as an unresolved placeholder. There is **no "Effective Date" paragraph, no authority
paragraph, and no severability paragraph** in the extracted pages: the text runs Purpose (¶¶1–2), Applicability (¶3),
Definitions (¶¶4–15), calculation requirements (¶¶16–32), Attachment — and stops. A search of the whole extraction for date
expressions returns only the date rules listed below and the two historical references noted in the next paragraph.

The **only calendar dates printed anywhere in A-830 are 1 January 2004**, and they all mark the same event: the cutover from the
1980 CSO basis (with elective select factors) to the 2001 CSO Mortality Table. (The month-year "December 1983" in the ¶9
definition and the experience years 1983–1986 in the ¶16 drafting note are historical references, not effective dates.) The
print states the cutover in two forms — "Effective January 1, 2004" for the new branch and "Prior to January 1, 2004" for the
old one, with ¶3.a.ii(b) and ¶5 instead saying "For contracts issued beginning" / "For contracts beginning" January 1, 2004.

### Grandfathering and transition language

- **Reentry grandfathering (¶3.a.i).** The appendix does not reach an individual policy issued on or after its effective date
  when that policy comes into being through the exercise of a reentry provision in an original policy that was issued before
  the effective date, has the same or a greater face amount, and guarantees the new policy's premium rates. The carve-out
  **propagates**: later policies issued by exercising that provision — or one derived from it — in the new policy are excluded
  too (p. 1206). Modelling consequence: reentry chains descending from a pre-effective-date policy stay on the old basis
  indefinitely, so the applicability flag is inherited at issue, not derived from the issue date alone.
- **Election lock-in (¶26.g).** A company that elects the attained-age-based YRT exemption must use it for every
  attained-age-based YRT policy issued on or after the appendix's effective date (p. 1217) — an all-or-nothing company-level
  election, not a per-policy one.
- **Post-issue unilateral changes (¶20).** Changes the insurer makes unilaterally after issue to guaranteed premiums, benefits,
  charges or credits, where the change is effective for more than one year, are handled by a triple-maximum rather than a
  transition rule (p. 1213); see Mechanics.
- **Secondary guarantees changed after issue (¶29.b).** A secondary guarantee the insurer changes unilaterally after issue is
  deemed to have been made at issue, and the ¶30/¶31 reserves are **recalculated from issue** to reflect the change (p. 1219).

### The exceptions (¶3.a) — six of them

1. **Reentry policies** (above).
2. **Short-secondary-guarantee universal life.** The appendix does not apply to a UL policy meeting **all three** of
   (pp. 1206–1207):
   (a) a secondary guarantee period, if there is one, of **five years or less**;
   (b) a specified premium for that period at least equal to the period's net level reserve premium, computed on the ¶9 CSO
   valuation tables at the applicable valuation interest rate — for contracts issued from 1/1/2004 that net level reserve
   premium uses **ultimate** 2001 CSO rates; and
   (c) an initial surrender charge of at least **100%** of the first-year annualized specified premium for the secondary
   guarantee period.
   A drafting note points the reader to ¶29 for what counts as a secondary guarantee, and a second drafting note at ¶29 confirms
   that UL and VUL policies with secondary guarantees fall outside the appendix where they meet the ¶3.a.ii requirements
   (p. 1219).
3. **Variable life** — any variable life policy whose amount or duration of insurance varies with separate account investment
   experience.
4. **Variable universal life** — same wording, applied to VUL.
5. **Group life certificates**, unless the certificate carries a schedule, stated or implied, of maximum gross premiums that
   must be paid to keep the coverage in force for longer than one year.
6. **Preneed policies**, which are governed by **A-817** instead.

### The conditions (¶3.b) — the two routing rules

- **Non-UL policies whose gross premiums or benefits, or both, are guaranteed to be nonlevel** are valued under **¶¶21–28**
  (p. 1207).
- **Flexible premium and fixed premium universal life** policies containing provisions that let a policyholder keep the policy
  in force over a **secondary guarantee period** are valued under **¶¶29–32** (p. 1207).

Nothing in ¶3.b routes a level-premium, level-benefit non-UL policy anywhere. Such a policy is still within the ¶3 scope
sentence and still subject to ¶2's declaration that the appendix's basic reserve method is CRVM for it, but the appendix
supplies no separate calculation paragraph for it. Treat that as an observation about the printed structure, not as a licence to
infer an outcome.

### 1. Definitions that carry weight (¶¶4–15)

| ¶ | Term | Operative content |

| 5 | **Contract segmentation method** | See §2 below — the core of the appendix. |
| 6 | **Deficiency reserves** | The positive part of (a) the minimum reserves under **Appendix A-820, ¶¶19–20** less (b) the basic reserves. |

### 2. The contract segmentation method (¶5) — segment determination

**Definition (PDF p. 1207).** The method cuts the span from issue to the policy's mandatory expiration into consecutive
segments. Each segment starts where the previous one ended (the first starts at inception) and ends at the end of the last
policy year that the length rule below assigns to it.

**Segment length rule (PDF p. 1208).** A segment's length is the smallest `t` at which `G(t)` exceeds `R(t)`. If `G(t)` never
exceeds `R(t)`, the segment runs from its start to the policy's mandatory expiration date.

**The two ratios** (the print sets these as stacked fractions; superscript/subscript levels are lost in the text layer, so
subscripts are shown in parentheses here):

```
                GP(x+k+t)
    G(t)  =  ---------------
               GP(x+k+t-1)

                q(x+k+t)
    R(t)  =  ---------------
               q(x+k+t-1)
```

**Symbols, as the print defines them (PDF p. 1208):**

- `x` = original issue age.
- `k` = years elapsed from the issue date to the start of the segment.
- `t` = 1, 2, …, restarting at 1 when each new segment begins.
- `GP(x+k+t-1)` = the guaranteed gross premium "per thousand of face amount" in segment-year `t`, with policy fees
  left out **only when they are level over the premium-paying period**. (So the premium indexed at attained age `x+k+t-1` is the
  premium falling due at the start of segment-year `t`, i.e. policy year `k+t`.)
- `q(x+k+t-1)` = the deficiency-reserve valuation mortality rate for policy year `k+t`, **except that where the deficiency
  reserve uses modified select rates, the unmodified select rates are used here**. The print defines only the denominator rate;
  the numerator `q(x+k+t)` is the same quantity one policy year later, which the text leaves implicit.

**The one-percent tolerance (PDF p. 1208).** At the company's option `R(t)` may be raised or lowered by one percent in any
policy year, but it may never fall below one.

So `R(t)` is company-elective within `max(1, R(t) ± 1%)`, elected **per policy year**. The drafting note gives the rationale
without arithmetic: the tolerance keeps premium rounding and similar effects from producing irrational segment lengths, the
worked illustration being a plan whose gross premiums are a ratio times the underlying ultimate mortality rates with the ratio
varying by issue age, where rounding to whole cents would otherwise produce segments longer than one year; with the tolerance, a
plan whose premiums track the valuation mortality table can be cut into one-year segments.

**Degenerate-premium conventions (PDF p. 1208).** Where `GP(x+k+t)` is positive and `GP(x+k+t-1)` is zero, `G(t)` is deemed to
be 1000; where both are zero, `G(t)` is deemed to be 0.

Implementation reading: a premium restarting after a zero-premium year forces `G = 1000`, which will exceed any `R`, so the
segment ends there; a run of zero premiums gives `G = 0`, which can never exceed `R ≥ 1`, so zero-premium years never break a
segment.

**Mortality basis for the segmentation calculation itself.** Per ¶5 (p. 1207), for contracts from 1/1/2004 all calculations are
made on 2001 CSO mortality, together with the optional ¶17 deficiency-reserve mortality standard where the company elects it.
The sentence sits inside the definition of the segmentation method, so the library reads "all calculations" as covering the
segmentation calculation — i.e. the segment boundaries move if the company elects X-factor mortality, **except** that the
unmodified select rates are used in `R(t)` where modified select rates are used for the deficiency reserve itself.

### 3. Segmented reserves (¶11)

**Construction (PDF p. 1209).** Segmented reserves use the segments the ¶5 method produces. Each is a prospective reserve —
PV of every future guaranteed benefit minus PV of every future net premium, both taken **to the policy's mandatory
expiration** — where within each segment the net premiums are one uniform percentage of that segment's guaranteed gross
premiums.

**The per-segment uniform percentage.** For each segment, the percentage is set so that at the **beginning of that segment** the
present value of net premiums within the segment equals:

```
   PV_seg(death benefits falling in the segment)
 + PV_seg(unusual guaranteed cash value (¶24) at the END of the segment, if any)
 - (unusual guaranteed cash value at the START of the segment, if any)
 + [first segment only]  EA = Item (a) - Item (b), where

     Item (a) = min( P_seg1 , P19(x+1) )
       P_seg1   = PV_issue(benefits of the FIRST SEGMENT after policy year 1)
                  / PV_issue(1 per year on each anniversary INSIDE THE FIRST SEGMENT at which a premium is due)
       P19(x+1) = net level annual premium of a 19-pay whole life plan for the same renewal-year equivalent
                  level amount, at issue age x+1

     Item (b) = net one-year term premium for the policy-year-1 benefits
```

That "excess of Item (a) over Item (b)" is the CRVM expense allowance, and the appendix's version of it is **segment-scoped**:
both the numerator benefits and the annuity denominator are restricted to the **first segment**, where the unitary version at
¶14 runs them to mandatory expiration. An implementation that reuses one expense-allowance routine for both must therefore
parameterise the horizon. The 19-pay-whole-life cap at age **x+1** is printed identically in ¶11 and ¶14.

**Interest rate cap (¶11.c, p. 1209).** The present-value interest rates for a policy may not exceed the maximum valuation
interest rate set for a guarantee duration equal to the **sum of the lengths of all the policy's segments**. Since ¶5 divides
the whole period from issue to mandatory expiration into successive segments, that sum spans the same period as the unitary rule
at ¶14.b (guarantee duration from issue to mandatory expiration) — the two are stated differently but reference the same span.

**Present-value scope (¶11.d, p. 1210).** Whether the segmented method is computing a basic or a deficiency reserve, its present
values take in future benefits and net premiums of **the current segment and every later segment**. A valuation at a date
inside segment 3 is not a segment-3-only calculation.

**Drafting note (p. 1210), which pre-empts three design questions.** Segmentation should not be confined to plans without cash
surrender values, or a company could escape it simply by building in small positive cash values; for plans that do have CSVs,
segments are to be set **by gross premium levels alone**; and segmented reserves are not to be tied to whether the unitary
terminal reserve is positive at a segment's end — holding the greater of the segmented and unitary reserves already makes any
such link unnecessary.

### 4. Unitary reserves (¶14)

**Construction (PDF p. 1210).** Unitary reserves are likewise prospective — PV of every future guaranteed benefit minus PV of
every future **modified** net premium, both run to the policy's mandatory expiration. The modified net premiums are a
**single** uniform percentage of the guaranteed gross premiums, fixed at issue by:

```
 PV_issue(modified net premiums) = PV_issue(all death benefits and pure endowments)
                                 + EA,   EA = Item (a) - Item (b)

   Item (a) = min( P_pol , P19(x+1) )
     P_pol    = PV_issue(benefits AFTER POLICY YEAR 1, to mandatory expiration)
                / PV_issue(1 per year on each POLICY anniversary at which a premium is due)
     P19(x+1) = the same 19-pay whole life cap at issue age x+1 as in ¶11

   Item (b) = net one-year term premium for the policy-year-1 benefits
```

Interest cap (¶14.b): the maximum valuation interest rate for a guarantee duration running from issue to the policy's mandatory
expiration.

The drafting note states the purpose plainly: to pin down as precisely as possible the method commonly called "unitary",
because A-820 does not define the term for policies with nonlevel premiums or benefits and its call for reserves on a method
consistent with A-820's principles has been read in different ways.

### 5. Basic reserves for nonlevel non-UL policies (¶21)

**The rule (PDF p. 1213).** The basic reserve is the **greater of the segmented reserve and the unitary reserve**, and both
legs must be computed on the same valuation mortality table and the same selection factors.

At the insurer's option, **one** of two adjustments may be made in calculating segmented reserves and net premiums:

- **(i)** for each segment, count the positive **unitary reserve** at the segment's end as a **pure endowment**, and deduct the
  positive unitary reserve at the segment's start from that segment's present value of guaranteed life insurance and endowment
  benefits (p. 1213); or
- **(ii)** the same construction using the **guaranteed cash surrender value** in place of the unitary reserve (p. 1214).

### 6. Deficiency reserves (¶17, ¶22)

**The quantity A construction (¶17, PDF p. 1211).** For each policy the deficiency reserve is the positive part of a quantity
**A** less the basic reserve. A is found by **re-running the basic reserve for the policy with the guaranteed gross premium put
in place of the net premium wherever the guaranteed gross premium is the lower of the two**.

This is more specific than "valuation net premiums in excess of gross": A is a **full re-run of the basic reserve** with the net
premium replaced, duration by duration, by the guaranteed gross premium wherever the gross is the smaller of the two. The
substitution is one-sided — where the gross premium exceeds the net premium the net premium stands.

**Which basis (¶22.a).** The deficiency reserve at any duration is calculated:

- on a **unitary** basis if the corresponding basic reserve under ¶21 is unitary;
- on a **segmented** basis if the corresponding basic reserve under ¶21 is segmented;
- on the **segmented** basis if the basic reserve is equal to both.

So the deficiency basis is a slave to the outcome of the ¶21 max, with the tie broken toward segmented.

**Trigger (¶22.b, p. 1214).** ¶22 reaches any policy whose guaranteed gross premium, at any duration, is below the matching
modified net premium — that net premium being computed by the basic-reserve method but **on the minimum valuation standards of
mortality (the ¶17 standard) and interest**. The comparison net premium is therefore on the *deficiency* standard, not
necessarily on the standard used for the basic reserve.

**Scope (¶22.c):** the excess of A over the basic reserve is taken for the current period and every remaining one, A being
built as ¶17 directs.

**Segment lengths (¶22.d):** where the deficiency reserve is on the segmented basis, A uses **the same segment lengths as the
segmented basic reserve**. Segments are not re-derived on the deficiency mortality basis.

### 7. Mortality standards and the X-factor mechanics (¶¶16–18)

**Basic reserves (¶16).** Prior to 1/1/2004, at company election for one or more specified plans, the minimum mortality standard
for basic reserves may use the 1980 CSO valuation tables with select mortality factors, the factors being (a) the ten-year select
factors referenced in A-820, (b) the select mortality factors in the Attachment, or (c) any later NAIC-adopted table of select
factors. **Effective 1/1/2004 the 2001 CSO Mortality Table is the minimum standard for basic reserves**, or any later valuation
table the NAIC adopts for the purpose after the 2001 CSO table's effective date (p. 1211).

A drafting note records the provenance of the Attachment factors (p. 1211): the duration 1–15 factors rest on Society of
Actuaries experience for **1983–1986**, split by sex and smoking status, projected forward **fifteen years** with SOA Projection
Scale A; a **50% margin** was then added, and the factors were graded into the 1980 CSO Tables over the following **five
durations** (16–20). The note judges 50% a reasonable margin that actual experience on sizeable blocks was unlikely to exceed.

**Deficiency reserves (¶17, pp. 1211–1213).** At company election for one or more specified plans, quantity A — and, "prior to
January 1, 2004", the corresponding net premiums that go into it — may rest on the 1980 CSO valuation tables with select
mortality factors (or a later NAIC-adopted valuation table). The elective factors are ¶17.a the A-820 ten-year select factors,
¶17.b the Attachment factors, ¶17.c X percent of the Attachment factors for first-segment durations, and ¶17.d any later
NAIC-adopted table of select factors for deficiency reserves. (As printed, the date qualifier sits mid-sentence and on its face
governs only the corresponding net premiums; the print does not say in terms that the quantity A election is pre-2004 only.)
**Effective 1/1/2004 the 2001 CSO Mortality Table is the minimum standard for deficiency reserves.** The X-factor rule follows:
where select rates are used, the rates for durations in the **first segment** may be multiplied by **X percent**, subject to
the conditions the print cross-refers as "Sections 17c. to iv. (a)" (p. 1211 — the cross-reference is garbled in the print;
it reads as ¶17.c through ¶17.c.vi.(a)).

An anti-cherry-picking rule comes with it: a compliance demonstration may not mix the results of 1980 CSO tests with those of
2001 CSO tests unless a regulation expressly requires the combination or the relevant Actuarial Standards of Practice make it
necessary.

**The X conditions (¶17.c, p. 1212), all six:**

| | Condition (paraphrased) |
|---|---|
| i | X may differ by policy year, policy form, underwriting class, issue age or any other policy characteristic expected to affect mortality |
| ii | At the **valuation interest rate used for basic reserves**, (a) ≥ (b), where (a) is the actuarial present value of future death benefits on the X-adjusted mortality and (b) is the same present value on "anticipated mortality experience without recognition of mortality improvement beyond the valuation date" |
| iii | The X-adjusted mortality rates are no lower than that anticipated experience (again with no improvement after the valuation date) **in each of the first five years after the valuation date** |
| iv | The appointed actuary **must raise** X at any valuation date where that is needed for ¶17.c to remain satisfied |
| v | The appointed actuary **may lower** X at any valuation date so long as ¶17.c stays satisfied |
| vi | The appointed actuary must expressly allow for the **adverse effect that any anticipated or actual gross premium increase has on expected mortality and lapsation** |

So the X test is a **two-limb** test: an aggregate present-value limb (ii) *and* a year-by-year floor over the next five years
(iii). Passing the PV test alone is not sufficient.

**Consequences of X < 100 (¶17.c.vi.(a), pp. 1212–1213).** Three requirements apply once X is below 100 percent "at any
duration for any policy":

1. the appointed actuary prepares, every year, an actuarial opinion and memorandum for the company that meets the
   **asset adequacy analysis** requirements of **Appendix A-822**;
2. the appointed actuary discloses in the **Regulatory Asset Adequacy Issues Summary** what effect it has if, in one or more
   interim periods, assets are insufficient to pay benefits and expenses and to set up statutory reserves; and
3. the appointed actuary gives an annual opinion, covering every policy subject to the appendix, on whether the X-adjusted
   mortality satisfies ¶17.c, backed by an actuarial report prepared under the applicable Actuarial Standards of Practice of the
   Actuarial Standards Board of the American Academy of Actuaries; and the **X factors must reflect anticipated future
   mortality, with no improvement after the valuation date, allowing for relevant emerging experience.**

The appendix prescribes **no X table, no floor value and no cap**; X is a company quantity constrained only by ¶17.c, and by
¶17.c.i it may be set above 100 as well as below.

**The first-segment restriction (¶18, p. 1213).** The rule governs basic and deficiency reserves alike: **a set of select
mortality factors may be used in the first segment only**. Where the first segment is shorter than ten years, though, the
A-820 ten-year select factors may continue after it, **up to the tenth policy year counted from issue**.

The drafting note gives the reasoning: select factors are barred after the first segment because a premium increase large
enough to start a new segment will drive up lapses and so worsen mortality afterwards; the note adds that, for policies with
reentry provisions, select factors may not be used in segments that begin after reentry **unless a new policy is actually
issued**. The ten-year carve-back is allowed because that deterioration is not expected to be material within the first ten
years.

**Policy fees (¶19, p. 1213).** Wherever a basic- or deficiency-reserve calculation uses the guaranteed gross premium, the
premium may be taken **without** policy fees, but only where the fee is a level dollar amount after policy year 1. Asymmetry
worth coding explicitly: for deficiency reserves the policy fees **may** be kept in the guaranteed gross premiums even when the
basic reserve calculation left them out.

### 8. Minimum values and floors (¶23)

- **Mean reserves (p. 1214):** the basic reserve is floored at **the tabular cost of insurance for the rest of the policy
  year**.
- **Mid-terminal reserves:** the floor is the tabular cost of insurance for the rest of the current modal period — **or up to
  the paid-to-date if that is later, but never past the next policy anniversary**.
- The tabular cost of insurance is computed on the valuation mortality table and interest rates of the segmented reserve; if
  select factors are used, they must be the **A-820 ten-year select factors**. Effective 1/1/2004 the table is the **ultimate**
  rates in the 2001 CSO Table.
- **The surrender-value floor (p. 1214).** Total reserves — basic, deficiency, and any reserves for supplemental benefits that
  would lapse when the contract ends — may never be less than what the policyowner would receive on termination, counting the
  cash surrender value of those supplemental benefits and **without deducting any policy loan**. Note this floor is on **total**
  reserves, not on the basic reserve alone.

### 9. Unusual pattern of guaranteed cash surrender values (¶24)

**The test (¶24.c, p. 1215).** A policy has an unusual pattern of guaranteed CSVs when some future guaranteed CSV is higher than
the guaranteed CSV of the year before by more than the total of:

```
   110% x (that year's scheduled gross premium)
 + 110% x (one year's interest on [ prior year's guaranteed CSV + scheduled gross premium ],
           at the NONFORFEITURE rate on which the policy's guaranteed CSVs are computed)
 +   5% x (first-policy-year surrender charge, if any)
```

**Before the first unusual value (¶24.a, p. 1215).** Until the first unusual guaranteed CSV, the reserve actually held is
floored at the reserve for an **n-year term policy plus a pure endowment** equal to that unusual CSV, with n the number of years
from issue to the date the unusual value is scheduled.

**After an unusual value (¶24.b, p. 1215).** After any unusual guaranteed CSV, the held reserve is floored by the same n-year
term-plus-pure-endowment construction aimed at the **next** unusual value, with the unusual CSV at the end of the previous
segment counted as a **net single premium**, where:

- `n` = years from the most recent unusual guaranteed CSV before the valuation date to whichever comes first of (a) the next
  unusual guaranteed CSV scheduled after the valuation date, if any, and (b) the policy's mandatory expiration date;
- the net premium for a given year in the n-year period = **net-to-gross ratio × the respective gross premium**; and
- the net-to-gross ratio is, with every PV taken at the start of the n-year period:

```
        PV(death benefits paid within the n years)
      + PV(next unusual guaranteed CSV, if any)
      - (last unusual guaranteed CSV, if any, falling at the start of the n years)
      -------------------------------------------------------------------------
        PV(scheduled gross premiums paid within the n years)
```

**Where this sits in the stack.** The drafting note (pp. 1214–1215) is explicit: the requirement stands apart from both the
segmentation and the unitary processes — **once the greater of the segmented and unitary reserves is known**, ¶24 lays a
further floor under the final reserve, its aim being to make sure large step-ups in guaranteed CSVs are adequately funded.
Unusual CSVs also enter the segmented reserve itself, via ¶11.a.ii and ¶11.a.iii.

### 10. Post-issue unilateral guarantee changes (¶20)

Where the insurer, after issue, unilaterally changes guaranteed gross premiums, benefits, charges or credits and the change
runs for more than one year from its date, the reserve is **the greatest of** three (p. 1213): (1) the reserve that ignores the
guarantee; (2) the reserve as if the guarantee had been made at issue; and (3) the reserve as if the policy had been issued on
the date of the guarantee. Three parallel valuations, not one.

### 11. The four exemptions (¶¶25–28)

**¶25 — Optional exemption for yearly renewable term reinsurance (p. 1216).** At company option:

- valuation net premium for each future policy year = **the tabular cost of insurance for that future year**;
- basic reserves never less than the tabular cost of insurance for the appropriate ¶23 period;
- deficiency reserves: for each policy year take the positive part of the valuation net premium less the **maximum guaranteed
  gross premium** for that year; the deficiency reserve is never less than the total of those excesses discounted to the
  valuation date;
- pre-2004 calculations use the maximum valuation interest rate and the 1980 CSO tables with or without ten-year select factors
  (or a later NAIC-adopted table); **from 1/1/2004 they use the ultimate rates in the 2001 CSO Table**;
- **definition (¶25.e):** a reinsurance agreement counts as YRT reinsurance for this paragraph "if only the mortality risk is
  reinsured";
- **Ceding-side consequence (¶25.f):** where the assuming company elects this exemption, the ceding company's reinsurance
  reserve credit is **capped at the reserve the assuming company holds** for the policies concerned.

The drafting note explains the exemption exists because traditional YRT reserves are already adequate, and without it YRT
reinsurance would be pulled into the more complex segmentation calculations.

**¶26 — Optional exemption for attained-age-based YRT life insurance policies (pp. 1216–1217).** Same four calculation rules as
¶25 (a–d), plus a definition and a lock-in:

- A policy qualifies if **both** (i) on **both the initial current premium scale and the guaranteed maximum premium scale**, the
  premium rates depend on the insured's attained age in such a way that the rate at a given attained age **does not depend on
  the policy's year of issue**, and (ii) on both scales those rates equal the rates charged to every insured of the same **sex,
  risk class, plan of insurance and attained age**.
- Policies that *become* attained-age-based after an initial period may use the approach after that period if the initial period
  is either the same length for every insured of the same sex, risk class and plan, **or** ends at a common attained age for all
  such insureds, and the ¶26.e conditions are met afterwards.
- **¶26.g lock-in:** if elected, the approach applies to **all** attained-age-based YRT policies issued on or after the effective
  date of the appendix.

**¶27 — Exemption from unitary reserves for certain n-year renewable term policies (pp. 1217–1218).** Neither the unitary basic
reserve nor the unitary deficiency reserve has to be computed where **all three** hold:

- (a) the policy is a chain of n-year periods — the first period and every renewal — with the same n throughout, except that the
  last renewal period may be shortened or lengthened to reach the expiry age **so long as it is under 10 years and under twice
  the length of the earlier n-year periods**; and within each period the rates are **level on the initial current scale and on
  the guaranteed maximum scale alike**;
- (b) prior to 1/1/2004, in every n-year period the guaranteed gross premiums are at least the matching net premiums on the 1980
  CSO Table, with or without the ten-year select factors — from 1/1/2004 the calculation uses ultimate 2001 CSO rates; and
- (c) **no policy year has a cash surrender value**.

Drafting note: without the exemption, some n-year renewable term writers could be made to hold more than n-year term reserves
even where gross premiums sit well above valuation mortality.

**¶28 — Exemption from unitary reserves for certain juvenile policies (p. 1218).** Tested on the initial current premium scale at
issue, all three required:

- (a) the insured is **24 or younger** at issue;
- (b) up to the end of the juvenile period — which must come **no later than age 25** — the gross premium and the death benefit
  are both level and the policy has **no cash surrender values**; and
- (c) after the juvenile period, gross premiums stay level for the rest of the premium-paying period and death benefits stay
  level for the rest of the policy's life.

Drafting note, and it matters: the jumping-juvenile design has traditionally been valued in two segments, and the exemption lets
that continue without a unitary calculation — **but within each segment both basic and deficiency reserves must still meet the
segmented-reserve requirements.** ¶27 and ¶28 remove the *unitary* leg only — the segmented calculation still runs.

### 12. Secondary guarantees on universal life (¶¶29–32)

**Heading as printed:** "Calculation of Minimum Valuation Standard for Flexible Premium and Fixed Premium Universal Life
Insurance Policies That Contain Provisions Resulting in the Ability of a Policyowner to Keep a Policy in Force Over a Secondary
Guarantee Period" (PDF p. 1218).

**What counts as a policy with a secondary guarantee (¶29.a, p. 1218).** Three cases:

1. a policy guaranteed to stay in force on its original schedule of benefits "subject only to the payment of specified
   premiums";
2. prior to 1/1/2004, a policy whose **minimum premium at some duration is below the matching one-year valuation premium**,
   the latter computed at the maximum valuation interest rate on the 1980 CSO valuation tables with or without ten-year select
   factors (or a later NAIC-adopted table) — from 1/1/2004 the one-year valuation premium uses ultimate 2001 CSO rates; or
3. any combination of the two.

Note the second limb: a policy can be swept in **without any explicit guarantee clause**, purely because its guaranteed cost
structure lets a minimum premium below the one-year valuation premium keep it alive.

**Secondary guarantee period, and multiple guarantees (¶29.b, p. 1219).** The secondary guarantee period is the span over which
the policy is guaranteed to stay in force on the strength of a secondary guarantee alone. Where a policy carries more than one
guarantee, the minimum reserve at a valuation date is **the largest of the minimum reserves computed for each unexpired
guarantee on its own**, each "ignoring all other secondary guarantees".

So each unexpired guarantee is valued **stand-alone**, and the maximum is taken — not a combined valuation. Unilateral post-issue
changes are deemed made at issue and force recalculation of ¶30/¶31 reserves from issue.

**The three premium definitions (¶29.c–f, p. 1219).**

- **Specified premiums** = the premiums named in the policy whose payment guarantees it stays in force on the original schedule
  of benefits, although without the guarantee they would not keep it in force **if maximum mortality and expense charges and
  minimum interest credits were applied and any surrender charge were taken**.
- **Minimum premium** for a policy year = the premium that, paid into a policy whose **account value is zero at the start of the
  year, leaves the account value at zero at the year's end**, computed on the policy cost factors — mortality charges, loads
  and expense charges among them — and the interest crediting rate, all **as guaranteed at issue**.
- **One-year valuation premium** = the net one-year premium for a given policy year on the original schedule of benefits.
  The one-year valuation premiums for **every policy year are fixed at issue**, and the ¶17.b, 17.c and 17.d select factors
  **may not be used** in computing them. They ought to reflect how often the fund is processed and the distribution-of-deaths
  assumption behind the fund's monthly mortality charges.

**Basic reserves for the secondary guarantees (¶30, p. 1219).** The basic reserve for the secondary guarantees is the
**segmented reserve over the secondary guarantee period**. Both the segments and the segmented reserve are computed with the
gross premiums replaced by the **specified premiums that keep the policy in force, or, where there are none, the minimum
premiums**, and the segments come from the ¶5 contract segmentation method.

That is the whole ULSG construction: substitute specified (or failing that, minimum) premiums for gross premiums, run the ¶5
segmentation on them, and take segmented reserves over the secondary guarantee period. **There is no unitary leg here** — ¶30
says "segmented reserves", not "the greater of segmented and unitary".

**Deficiency reserves for the secondary guarantees (¶31, p. 1219).** Computed over the secondary guarantee period **by the ¶22
method**, with gross premiums again set to specified premiums or otherwise minimum premiums.

**Minimum reserves during the secondary guarantee period (¶32, p. 1219).** The greater of:

- (a) the basic reserve for the secondary guarantees **plus** any deficiency reserve for them; or
- (b) the minimum reserves required by "other appendices governing universal life plans".

Limb (b) is an unnamed cross-reference — the appendix does not say which appendix item it means. Do not resolve it to A-585 on
this text alone.

### 13. The Attachment — select mortality factors

**Explanatory note (PDF p. 1220, printed A830-15).** Under the heading "SELECT MORTALITY FACTORS" the Attachment makes four

- the tables are the base factors to which the percentages of ¶¶16.b, 17.b and 17.c are applied (see "Gaps and caveats" on how

- there are six of them — male aggregate, male nonsmoker, male smoker, female aggregate, female nonsmoker, female smoker;

### Ambiguities and extraction artefacts

- **The garbled X-factor cross-reference.** ¶17 makes the X-percent option subject to the conditions in "Sections 17c. to iv.
  (a)" (p. 1211). The first extraction showed the word split as "Sectio ns", a text-layer artefact that the `pdftotext -layout`
  text layer used for the 2026-10-25 revision does not reproduce. The reference itself is malformed: the conditions run to
  ¶17.c.vi, and the "(a)" sub-block sits under ¶17.c.vi. The intended reading is ¶17.c in its entirety including
  ¶17.c.vi.(a). Note also that the appendix says "Sections" here even though it is numbered in paragraphs — evidence that this
  sentence was carried over from a differently structured source text. Flag rather than resolve.
- **Duplicated clause at the 1207/1208 page break.** ¶5 brings in the optional ¶17 deficiency-reserve mortality standard "if
  elected" **twice**, once before and once after the parenthetical about other NAIC-adopted tables. This looks like a drafting
  or typesetting duplication carried into the print; it does not change the meaning.
- **Lost superscripts and broken formula layout.** The `G(t)` and `R(t)` definitions are set as stacked fractions with
  subscripted variables. In the text layer the subscripts drop to the baseline ("GPx+k+t", "qx+k+t-1") and the `R(t)` fraction
  is interleaved line-by-line with the one-percent tolerance note (p. 1208); the first extraction also showed that note
  letter-spaced mid-formula ("o p t i o n ,  b u t  R"), which the `pdftotext -layout` text layer does not. The formulas
  above were reconstructed from that layout in the library's notation; the *relationships* are unambiguous but a reader wanting
  the exact typography must go to the PDF.
- **Word-internal spaces throughout.** The first extraction's text layer inserted spurious spaces mid-word at line-wrap
  points: "vari able", "co verage", "begi nning", "Insuranc e", "defici ency", "se lect", "segm ent", "E ffective", "Janu ary",
  "mi nimum", "P olicies", "endo wment", "annivers ary", "accord ance", "polic y". These are extraction artefacts, not the
  printed text.
  Since the 2026-10-25 revision the file paraphrases instead of quoting at length, and its few short quotations are given as
  printed, without the stray spaces; the `pdftotext -layout` text layer used for that revision does not show the splits.
- **"Attachment 1" versus "Attachment".** ¶¶16.b, 17.b and 17.c all refer to "the select mortality factors in **Attachment 1** of
  this appendix", but the attachment is headed simply "**Attachment**" with no number. There is only one attachment, so the
  referent is unambiguous.
- **The Attachment header conflates two uses.** It calls the tables the bases to which "the respective percentage of paragraphs
  16.b., 17.b. and 17.c." is applied (p. 1220). In fact ¶¶16.b and 17.b apply the tabulated factors themselves, while ¶17.c
  applies *X percent of* them — the header's phrasing obscures the distinction.
- **The `q` numerator is not defined.** ¶5 defines `q(x+k+t-1)` explicitly but leaves `q(x+k+t)` to inference.
- **Table structure.** Duration columns run 1–19 with a single terminal `20+`; issue-age rows are banded `0-15` and `85+` at the
  ends. Two blank spacer lines appear at the foot of the first page of each two-page table; they carry no data. When this file
  still carried the tables, all six parsed to exactly 71 rows × 20 values with no ragged rows, a strong indication the numeric
  extraction was clean, but the values were **not** checked against an independent copy. The file no longer reproduces them
  (§13); read them in the manual.
- **Step blocks are as printed.** From issue age 70 to 84 every table repeats one sequence of factors by duration (the factor
  depends on duration alone there; the three male tables share one such sequence and the three female tables another), cut
  short by the staircase of 100s, and the rows just below age 70 step into that pattern in flat blocks of equal factors rather
  than by a smooth grade — for example the triangular block at issue ages 61–64, durations 7–10, on the second page of each
  table (the issue-age 61, duration 10 cell that an earlier revision singled out sits in it). That step structure is as
  printed and should not be "corrected" by an implementer who assumes it is an artefact.
- **Scope observation, not a derivation.** ¶3 reaches all life insurance policies subject to the six exceptions, but ¶3.b routes
  only nonlevel non-UL policies (¶¶21–28) and UL-with-secondary-guarantee policies (¶¶29–32) to a calculation. Level-premium,
  level-benefit non-UL business is inside the scope sentence and inside ¶2's CRVM declaration with no calculation paragraph of
  its own. That is what the text says; what it implies for a whole life valuation is not resolved here.
